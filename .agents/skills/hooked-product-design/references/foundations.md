# Foundations and habit suitability

Book basis: introduction and chapter 1. Diagnostic actions are operational synthesis.

## Define the behavior

A habit is a cue-associated behavior performed with little deliberate thought. A Hook connects a recurring need with a solution through repeated cycles. Retention can also reflect obligation, incentives, contracts, or lack of alternatives; it does not alone demonstrate habit.

Write: **For [user], when [situation/need], doing [observable behavior] delivers [benefit] at [natural cadence].** Avoid vague goals such as engagement or opening the app. In a learning game, completing a useful exercise may be the beneficial behavior; collecting a login bonus may only be a proxy.

Separate first-use activation, repeated valuable use, and unprompted return. Each needs different interventions; good onboarding cannot compensate for a poor recurring experience.

## The Habit Zone

The book combines frequency and perceived utility relative to alternatives. More frequent opportunities reinforce associations; greater utility may support less frequent behavior. Very rare behavior should not be forced into daily use. The diagram has no universal numeric boundary or guaranteed formation period.

Episodic purchases, emergency support, and one-off checkout may need excellent completion rather than habit. A weekly hobby may support weekly rhythm. Useful task software can reduce time in the app while increasing outcomes.

## Business implications

The book discusses customer lifetime value, pricing flexibility, growth through repeated sharing opportunities, and competitive advantages from routines and stored value. Use these as business hypotheses; they do not justify harmful usage, higher prices, difficult exit, or unsolicited invitations.

Viral cycle time concerns how quickly an invitation leads to another participating user. Frequent useful interaction can create more opportunities, but acquisition economics and monetization need independent validation. Hooked is not a complete business model.

## Vitamins, relief, and old habits

An optional enjoyment can become a familiar source of relief. Discover its underlying benefit rather than treating vitamin/painkiller as a rigid market test. Never create a problem to sell relief.

Established routines compete with new ones. Familiar patterns and migration of useful work reduce change costs. The book's dramatic superiority comparisons are illustrations, not a requirement that a product be numerically nine times better.

## Suitability decision

State: suitable recurring behavior; plausible but needs evidence; useful episodic behavior; or poor/harmful fit. Include cadence, alternative, user benefit, uncertainty, and smallest next investigation. If habits are a poor fit, still improve the requested outcome.
