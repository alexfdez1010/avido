# Discover needs and behavior

Book basis: chapters 2, 4, and 8. Prompts, evidence recording, and segmentation are practical extensions.

## Research a recent episode

Internal triggers are learned associations with thoughts, emotions, or routines. External cues contain information about the next action. An icon is external; the uncertainty that makes someone reach for it may be internal.

Ask about an actual recent occasion rather than hypothetical feature preferences:

- What were you doing immediately before use? Where, with whom, on which device?
- What changed that made you want to act? What happens if you do nothing?
- What did you try first? What did the old solution do well?
- Show the steps. Where did you hesitate or change course?
- What did you expect? Did you get it? What satisfied or disappointed you?
- Did you save, follow, annotate, schedule, or learn anything useful later?
- What brought you back next time? What made you stop?
- When would a reminder help or intrude?

Do not lead with boredom, loneliness, or anxiety. Positive anticipation and routines also matter. Describe emotions as participant statements or hypotheses, never diagnoses.

## Uncover the need beneath a request

Use repeated why/how questions to investigate purpose. Five whys is a prompt, not a requirement to ask exactly five questions or invent a psychological story. Stop when answers lack support; observe or mark the gap.

Record situation, prior action, expressed feeling/goal, workaround, frequency, desired outcome, confidence, and counterexamples. Distinguish declared preferences from observed actions without dismissing either. Discrepancies generate questions, not proof of hidden motivation.

## Narrative and evidence ledger

Write: **In [context], [person] notices [need]. They currently [workaround]. They try [action], expect [result], then [useful follow-up].** Add constraints only when supported.

| User quits after seeing many modes | Observed task | Choice burden may block action | Medium     | Guided starting option |

Do not fabricate analytics, interviews, or causal conclusions. With limited access, label changes as hypotheses and identify evidence that would resolve uncertainty.

## Research size and opportunity observation

The book suggests five reward interviews, observing three people outside one's usual circle, and a week of self-observation. These are starting exercises, not statistical sufficiency. Treat self, users, and non-users as different sources. Expand when consequential decisions or conflicting evidence warrant it.

For first-screen apps and unusual early-adopter workflows, investigate recurring needs and removable steps. Avoid copying surface mechanics without context.
