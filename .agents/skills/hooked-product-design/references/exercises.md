# Chapter exercises translated into practical work

These prompts paraphrase and operationalize the book's chapter-end exercises. They are not verbatim text. Use only the exercise matching the current problem; use the full sequence for a requested workshop.

## Chapter 1 — Choose a suitable habit

Write down the business-relevant routine, user problem, existing solution and its shortcoming, expected natural frequency, and observable target behavior. Separate useful behavior from a business proxy. Produce a suitability statement with assumptions.

Output: **[User] repeatedly encounters [need], currently [workaround], and would benefit from [behavior/result] at [cadence].**

## Chapter 2 — Find and align triggers

1. Identify the intended user and what they do immediately before the desired behavior.
2. Generate three candidate internal triggers using careful why/how investigation; label unverified emotions.
3. Choose the most frequent supported relevant need.
4. Write a need-to-action narrative: when the person experiences the need, what small first action helps?
5. Identify usable places and moments for external cues close to that need.
6. Generate three conventional cue approaches and three unconventional ones; mark feasibility, privacy, permissions, and relevance.

Output: a selected trigger contract and alternatives, not six channels to deploy.

## Chapter 3 — Simplify action

Trace the whole path from internal need to expected reward. Count steps, decisions, waits, and input. Compare with existing alternatives.

Identify constraints across time, money, physical effort, mental effort, social appropriateness, and disruption of routines. Generate three testable simplifications. Consider the chapter's scarcity, framing, anchoring, and endowed-progress examples only when truthful and relevant.

Output: before/after path and the most supported simplification. Explain what necessary information/control remains.

## Chapter 4 — Deliver the right reward

Begin with five open-ended customer conversations about satisfaction, encouragement, surprise, and disappointment if such research is authorized and feasible. Treat five as a workshop starting point, not representative proof.

Review the actual outcome that resolves the need. Generate three meaningful reward ideas across tribe, hunt, and self. Decide whether each fits the user purpose and how novelty is sustained or honestly completed. Preserve freedom to choose.

Output: a reward hypothesis, relevant variation, reliable core promise, and evidence needed.

## Chapter 5 — Design investment

Inspect the current flow for work that improves next use. Generate three small investment candidates considering both stored value and preparation of a relevant next cue. Examine content, data, relationships/followers, reputation, and skill.

Determine the delay between investment and the next useful occasion. Reduce unnecessary delay without sending cues before the need exists. Stage effort in proportion to experienced value.

Output: investment ask, why now, stored benefit, persistence, next-use pathway, cue if appropriate, and edit/decline route.

## Chapter 6 — Reflect on influence

Ask the responsible humans whether they would use the product and believe it improves users' lives. Consider the behavior being encouraged, direct knowledge of the need, and pride or concern about the influence. Do not have the AI fabricate a first-person answer.

Use the matrix to prompt research/reflection; do not turn it into a simplistic verdict on specialist products or chosen entertainment. State benefit, practical autonomy, likely downside, and relevant response to overuse.

Output: a concise reflection and any necessary design correction. The exercise does not impose a universal approval gate.

## Chapter 7 — Study an integrated example

This chapter's case study has no separate chapter-end action sequence in the provided edition. As an extension, map the Bible app's accessibility, short reading units, chosen plans/cues, meaningful interpretation, completion feedback, and annotations to the four phases.

Compare mechanisms with the actual product's needs. Identify one transferable pattern and one element that should not be copied. Do not transfer historical metrics or assumptions about religious motivation.

Output: a contextual pattern comparison; see [case patterns](case-patterns.md) only if needed.

## Chapter 8 — Habit Testing and opportunities

1. Identify repeated useful users at an appropriate cadence, codify candidate paths, and modify/test a narrow change.
2. Observe your behavior and emotions over a week: what prompted use, whether usage matched intent, and what onboarding/return/investment could improve.
3. Observe three people outside your usual circle using first-screen apps as they normally do; look for unnecessary steps and early-adopter behaviors.
4. Brainstorm five interface changes that might create opportunities or threats, distinguishing feasible improvements from speculation.

Output: candidate habit path, narrow test, and ranked opportunity cards. The observation counts are starting exercises, not statistical adequacy.

## Five-question review

Paraphrasing chapter 6: what recurring need is being relieved; what external cue leads to use; what smallest action earns the result; what meaningful reward supports return; and what contribution improves next use or prepares the next cue?

Answer with evidence and uncertainty. A filled canvas is not proof that a habit exists. Stop the workshop at the requested deliverable rather than automatically implementing every idea.
